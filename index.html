Bread goes stale. Steel rusts. Money just sits there.

Silvio Gesell's argument in 1916 was that this asymmetry is the whole problem. Everything real decays, so anyone holding goods is under pressure to trade. Money doesn't decay, so whoever holds it can simply wait — and in a downturn, waiting is exactly what everyone does at once.

His fix was blunt: make money rot too.

It never quite reaches zero — a charge, not a confiscation. Half of it is gone in a bit under six years. Spend it and the charge stops; the clock restarts for whoever you gave it to.

It was tried, it worked, and it was banned

In July 1932 the Austrian town of Wörgl was dying. Of 4,200 people, around 1,500 had no work, the cement plant had closed, and the town was too broke to fix its roads.

The mayor, a former railway worker named Michael Unterguggenberger, issued "Certified Compensation Bills" that lost 1% of face value every month. To keep a note current you bought a stamp and stuck it on the back. Nobody wanted to be holding one at month's end.

The scrip changed hands nine to ten times faster than the national schilling. The town paved streets, built a bridge and a ski jump. Unemployment in Wörgl fell 16% while it rose 19% across Austria. Irving Fisher and Keynes both took an interest, and roughly two hundred other towns queued up to copy it.

Austria's central bank shut it down in 1933, on the grounds that issuing currency is the state's business alone.

The part that matters more than the success

American towns copied the idea during the Depression and several set the decay far higher — around 2% a week. At that rate the money was simply refused. People wouldn't take it, so it stopped being money at all.

That failure is the more useful data point, and it's why this contract has a hard ceiling of 5% per period written in as a constant nobody can change, including whoever deployed it. Demurrage has a working range, and outside it the currency dies.

Charge at the moment of touchEach balance carries its own clock. The decay is computed when someone interacts with it — which is exactly how the stamp worked in 1932.

An untouched balance is already smaller in every view the contract offers. Settling doesn't take anything from you — it just writes down what was true anyway, and pays the commons.

Where the decay goes

In Wörgl the stamp money went to the town and paid for roadworks. Here it goes to a commons address fixed at deployment, which can never be changed — not by the issuer, not by anyone. A commons someone can redirect later isn't a commons.

Anyone can settle anyone's balance. That sounds like an attack until you notice it can only take what was already lost and can only send it to that one fixed address. It means an abandoned wallet still funds the commons instead of sitting in limbo.

Why nobody ships this

Because holders hate it, and holders are who you're selling to. Every token launch is implicitly a pitch that the number will go up if you sit still. This is the opposite pitch, and it's a bad one — which is precisely what makes it worth building once.

It's also worth saying that the Wörgl result is contested. Sceptics point out that the town was simultaneously spending on public works, that some notes were hoarded anyway as collectibles, and that a fourteen-month experiment in a town of four thousand proves less than its advocates claim. It's a striking piece of history, not a settled economic result.

What you should not do with it

Don't hold it. Not a warning — a description. This is engineered to lose value in your hands and it will do exactly that.

It is not an investment. There's no scenario where the number goes up. Anyone framing it as one has misread the entire premise.

Rounding always favours the contract. Integer division floors, so a very small balance can decay to nothing. Dust behaves like dust.

Issuance is one-way when closed. Once the issuer calls it, no further units can exist, ever.

Unaudited, on a testnet. The decay math is checked against a simulation covering the historical rate, the ceiling, part-periods and 5,000 randomized balances with nothing created or destroyed. That isn't a professional review.
